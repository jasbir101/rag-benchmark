# Residential solar panels

Rooftop solar panels convert sunlight into direct current electricity using photovoltaic cells, usually made of crystalline silicon. Most homes use alternating current, so an inverter converts the panels' output before it reaches household circuits or the grid.

There are two common inverter layouts. A string inverter connects a series of panels to a single central unit; it is cheaper but the whole string's output can be dragged down by one shaded panel. Microinverters or power optimisers sit behind each panel, so shade on one panel affects only that panel. They cost more but suit roofs with chimneys, trees or several orientations.

Orientation and tilt determine how much energy a system produces. In the northern hemisphere, panels facing south collect the most energy over a year; in the southern hemisphere, north-facing panels do. A tilt close to the site's latitude is a good starting point, although east and west roofs still produce useful power, especially in the morning and evening.

Panel efficiency, the share of sunlight converted to electricity, is typically 19 to 23 percent for modern residential modules. Efficiency falls slightly as panels get hot, which is why output on a cool, clear spring day can beat a scorching summer afternoon.

Many utilities offer net metering: when panels produce more than the house uses, surplus electricity flows to the grid and earns a credit that offsets later consumption. A home battery stores surplus energy for the evening instead, and can keep essential circuits running during a power cut if the system is designed for backup.

Panels need little maintenance. Rain usually keeps them clean enough, and most manufacturers warrant at least 80 percent of the original output after 25 years.
