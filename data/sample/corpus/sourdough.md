# Keeping a sourdough starter

A sourdough starter is a culture of wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria living in a paste of flour and water. The yeast produces carbon dioxide that leavens bread, and the bacteria produce the acids that give sourdough its tang.

To create a starter, mix equal weights of whole-grain flour and water, cover the jar loosely and keep it somewhere around 24 to 26 degrees Celsius. Every day, discard most of the mixture and feed what remains with fresh flour and water. After five to ten days the starter should rise predictably and smell pleasantly sour rather than like nail-polish remover.

Feeding ratios control how quickly a starter peaks. A 1:1:1 feed (one part starter, one part flour, one part water by weight) peaks in four to six hours at room temperature. A 1:5:5 feed slows things down and is handy when you want the starter ready the next morning.

A starter is ready to bake with when it has at least doubled in volume and its surface is domed and full of bubbles. A small spoonful that floats in water is a rough sign of enough gas, although the float test is not reliable for very wet or whole-grain starters.

Between bakes, the starter can live in the refrigerator and be fed about once a week. Cold slows fermentation dramatically. Before baking, give it one or two feeds at room temperature so it becomes active again.

A layer of grey liquid on top, often called hooch, means the starter is hungry. Pour it off or stir it in, then feed. Pink or orange streaks, on the other hand, indicate contamination, and the starter should be thrown away.
