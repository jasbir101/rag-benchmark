# Rainwater harvesting at home

Rainwater harvesting collects runoff from a roof and stores it for later use, most often for watering gardens, washing cars or flushing toilets. It reduces demand on mains water and the amount of stormwater flowing into drains during heavy rain.

The potential yield is easy to estimate. Each millimetre of rain falling on one square metre of roof produces about one litre of water. A 100 square metre roof in a place with 800 millimetres of annual rainfall could therefore collect up to 80,000 litres a year, although losses from evaporation, splashing and first-flush diversion reduce the usable amount by perhaps 10 to 20 percent.

A basic system has gutters and downpipes, a leaf screen, a first-flush diverter and a storage tank. The first-flush diverter discards the first few litres of each rainfall, which carry most of the dust, bird droppings and debris washed off the roof. Tanks should be opaque to stop algae growing and sealed with fine mesh so that mosquitoes cannot breed inside.

Sizing the tank is a balance between cost and how long the household must bridge dry spells. A small garden may only need a 200 to 500 litre barrel, while supplying toilets and laundry year-round may justify several thousand litres.

Untreated rainwater is generally not safe to drink. Roof materials, animal droppings and airborne pollution can all contaminate it, so drinking-water systems need filtration and disinfection and should follow local regulations. For garden use, however, rainwater is ideal: it is soft, free of chlorine, and plants respond well to it.

Clean gutters and screens at least twice a year, and check the tank inlet and overflow after major storms.
