# Life in tide pools

Tide pools form on rocky shores where seawater is trapped in hollows as the tide goes out. Twice a day their inhabitants are cut off from the open ocean and must cope with rapid changes in temperature, salinity and oxygen.

The shore is divided into zones by how long each part spends underwater. The splash zone at the top is only wetted by spray and is home to hardy lichens and periwinkle snails. The high intertidal zone is exposed for most of the day; barnacles and limpets dominate there, clamping shut or sealing themselves to the rock to avoid drying out. The middle zone supports mussel beds, sea anemones and seaweeds. The low intertidal zone is uncovered only at the lowest tides and holds the richest community, including sea stars, sea urchins and many fish.

Sea stars are important predators. Experiments in which ochre sea stars were removed from a shore showed mussels spreading until they crowded out most other species. Because one predator held the whole community in balance, the sea star became the classic example of a keystone species.

Anemones look like flowers but are animals related to jellyfish. They catch prey with stinging tentacles and can close into a tight blob at low tide to hold in water.

Visiting tide pools is best at a low or negative tide, which tide tables list in advance. Step only on bare rock, since seaweed and barnacles are slippery and easily crushed. Look without lifting animals from the rock, and if you turn over a stone, put it back exactly as it was, because the creatures underneath depend on that shade and moisture.
