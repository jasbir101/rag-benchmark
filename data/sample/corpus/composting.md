# Home composting

Composting turns kitchen scraps and garden waste into a dark, crumbly soil amendment. The process is driven by bacteria, fungi and small invertebrates that break organic matter down when they have the right mix of food, air and moisture.

A healthy pile balances "greens" and "browns". Greens are nitrogen-rich materials such as vegetable peelings, coffee grounds and fresh grass clippings. Browns are carbon-rich materials such as dry leaves, shredded cardboard and straw. A common rule of thumb is roughly three parts browns to one part greens by volume. Too many greens make the pile wet and smelly; too many browns make it slow.

Moisture matters as much as the mix. The pile should feel like a wrung-out sponge. If it is dry, decomposition stalls; if it is soggy, air cannot reach the microbes and the pile turns anaerobic, which is what produces the rotten-egg smell. Turning the pile every week or two with a garden fork adds oxygen and speeds things up.

A well-managed hot pile can reach 55 to 65 degrees Celsius in its centre. That heat kills most weed seeds and many plant pathogens. Cold composting, where material is simply added over time and left alone, also works but can take a year or more.

Avoid adding meat, dairy, oily food and pet waste to a backyard bin, because they attract pests and can carry pathogens. Diseased plants and weeds that have gone to seed are also best kept out unless the pile reliably gets hot.

Finished compost smells earthy, and the original materials are no longer recognisable. Screen it to remove large pieces, which can go back into the next batch, and mix the rest into garden beds or use it as a top dressing for lawns.
